# Design Aesthetics

Source: [Figma — What are design aesthetics?](https://www.figma.com/resource-library/what-are-design-aesthetics/)

## Definition

Design aesthetics are the visual elements — shape, balance, white space, color
scheme, typography — that designers use to stir audience emotion. Done right, this
isn't just decoration: it creates designs that are pleasing to the eye *and* meet the
user's actual wants and needs. Apple and Google are the standard reference for pairing
eye-catching design with genuinely useful UX and interaction design — the aesthetic
choices support the function rather than fighting it.

## Why it matters

Aesthetics affect real business outcomes, not just taste:
- **First impressions** — a strong aesthetic makes users want to browse and learn
  more.
- **Engagement** — unique, intuitive design concepts tap into how people already
  think and feel, rather than asking them to adapt to the product.
- **Brand identity and credibility** — a simple, consistent aesthetic clarifies what a
  company is actually saying.

## The aesthetic-usability effect

A 1995 Hitachi Design Center study found that users perceive visually appealing
designs as *easier to use* — even when they objectively aren't. This is a genuinely
useful (and slightly dangerous) fact:
- Useful: investment in visual polish pays off in perceived usability, and products
  combining aesthetic design with real usability win loyal brand advocates.
- Dangerous: a polished design can *mask* real usability problems rather than solve
  them. Aesthetic effort is not a substitute for actual usability testing — treat it
  as a complement.

## A little design history worth having on hand

- **Late 19th century — the Aesthetic Movement**: creative expression and global
  influence, a reaction against grim industrial factories and dim Victorian interiors.
  William Morris's lush color palettes and prints (floral, songbird motifs) are still
  referenced today.
- **1920s — Art Deco**: eye-catching color and industrial-chic geometric shapes.
- **Mid-century — Charles and Ray Eames**: modern standards for industrial design;
  clean lines and gentle curves, balancing form with function (their lounge chair and
  ottoman is the canonical example).
- **Apple today**: elegant, minimalist design as the throughline for a loyal, growing
  customer base — a contemporary continuation of the same "aesthetics in service of
  usability" idea.

## Five best practices

1. **Know your audience.** Understand users' backgrounds, belief systems, behaviors,
   preferences, and pain points before making aesthetic choices — style decisions
   should follow from who's actually using this, not from personal taste.
2. **Make it useful.** Form should support content and function, not compete with it.
   Consistent UX lets users easily find what they're looking for regardless of how
   visually rich the design is.
3. **Keep it simple.** Start with minimalism — include only relevant elements. Using
   an established design system (e.g. Google's Material Design) is a shortcut to
   simple, intuitive experiences rather than reinventing conventions from scratch.
4. **Apply core design principles deliberately.** Gestalt principles (how users make
   sense of patterns and images) explain *why* organizing visual information a certain
   way works — use that structure rather than relying on instinct alone.
5. **Consider context.** Good design accounts for users' real-world challenges — where
   and how they'll actually encounter and use this, not an idealized viewing
   condition.
