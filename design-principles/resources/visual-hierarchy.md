# Visual Hierarchy

Source: [Figma — What is visual hierarchy](https://www.figma.com/resource-library/what-is-visual-hierarchy/)

## Why it matters

Visual hierarchy is the practice of arranging elements in order of importance so a
user's attention lands where it needs to. The core problem it solves: "If everything
looks the same, then you see nothing" (Miguel Cardona, Figma). A screen with no
hierarchy forces the user to read everything before they can find anything — a slide
that's too dense, a page with no clear call to action, a form with too many
undifferentiated options.

Done well, hierarchy lets people understand information *intuitively*, the way a
glance at a movie poster tells you what the movie is about, who's in it, and when it
opens — with the rating and studio affiliation still there, just visually quieter.

Visual hierarchy draws on Gestalt psychology — how the mind creates order out of
chaos and groups separate elements into a whole. The principles below are the levers
designers pull to make that grouping happen on purpose instead of by accident.

## The seven levers

**Alignment** — the placement of one element relative to others in a group. Aligned
elements read as related to each other; the relationship is inferred from position
alone, before any label or color says so.

**Color** — described in hue, lightness, and saturation, but tricky because people
perceive color differently from each other. Cardona's specific advice: think in terms
of *luminance and brilliance*, not just hue. Practical version — use a pre-set palette
with good contrast (a color wheel generator helps here) rather than picking colors
screen by screen.

**Contrast** — juxtaposing notably different elements that sit close together.
Designers often pair warm/cool or complementary colors both for visual appeal and to
boost contrast ratios for accessibility — the two goals reinforce each other here,
they don't trade off.

**Proximity** — how near or far items are from each other. "Chunking" related objects
together is proximity in action; it's often the single fastest way to make a busy
layout legible.

**Size** — bigger reads as more important. This has a real accessibility dimension,
not just an aesthetic one: "If you don't provide low-vision users the opportunity to
have agency over text and image size, you're making your content difficult to read
and understand" (Cardona). Don't treat size purely as a layout-fitting variable.

**Texture** — how a surface feels, or is perceived to feel. Texture lets you "ascribe
meaning to design" and can create highlights without relying on color at all. Caution:
using texture to mimic real-world tactile material (skeuomorphism) reads as dated or
literal unless it's a deliberate choice — most digital designers avoid texture
entirely, which Cardona thinks is a missed opportunity when it's used well.

**Time** — screens change, react, and transform in ways print never could. Temporal
design (motion, transitions, sequencing over time) is a hierarchy lever unique to
digital work — it doesn't exist in a static composition.

## The example worth knowing

Cardona's go-to reference is Massimo and Lella Vignelli: "The thing about the
Vignellis and their work is the way they employ scale and hierarchy... there's
something for you based on your relationship to that object. For every degree of
distance that you have from a design, there is an engagement point." Worth a look if
you want to see hierarchy used at a level beyond UI — books, posters, spaces,
furniture, all built on the same interplay of scale.

## Five practical takeaways

1. **Know your end user and their context.** A mobile design is being held by someone
   in a specific situation — rushed, one-handed with a bag of groceries, or relaxed
   with full attention. Don't assume uniform distance/attention across all users.
2. **The medium determines which principle matters most.** A billboard lives or dies
   on scale; a mobile app might lean harder on proximity and contrast. Don't apply the
   same weighting everywhere.
3. **Question, experiment, test, iterate.** When everything looks the same, nothing
   stands out — bold moves are often correct moves. Treat hierarchy choices as
   testable hypotheses, not one-shot decisions.
4. **A little goes a long way.** The most common failure mode is trying to do too
   much. Apple and Google's long-standing use of white/negative space is worth
   revisiting as a default instinct, not just an occasional technique.
5. **Consider progressive disclosure.** Break information or steps into digestible
   chunks across multiple screens instead of one dense one — this lowers the odds
   someone feels overwhelmed. Applies to onboarding flows, complex forms, or any
   screen trying to do too many jobs at once.
